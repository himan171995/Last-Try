    The page

    What Drik Panchang shows

    Each page is a timetable for one date and one city. The title follows the pattern “[date] Pancha Pakshi Activities for [city]”. Many copies in search results use New Delhi. The timetable has a day-time section and a night-time section. Each section is a run of time slots.

    The Panchang day runs from sunrise to the next sunrise. Slots after midnight carry the next calendar date.

    Pancha Pakshi Shastra comes from the Tamil Siddha tradition, and a Sanskrit text on it is credited to the sage Agastya. Each person has one bird, worked out from the nakshatra the Moon was in at birth and the paksha (waxing or waning fortnight). The page then judges every slot of the day against that bird.

    Every bird passes through all five states over a day. Strength drops by one fifth at each step down the list. A slot where your bird is Ruling or Eating is treated as strong. A slot where it is Sleeping or Dying is treated as weak. The page's Rating column gives its own verdict for each slot.

    Strength values: VedAstro (Part 3) and Jayachandran et al. in IJMRME. Both use the same 1.0 to 0.2 scale.

    Your bird

    Finding your bird

    Your bird comes from two facts: the nakshatra the Moon was in when you were born, and whether you were born in the waxing (Shukla) or waning (Krishna) fortnight. The same nakshatra gives a different bird in each fortnight. The Moon changes nakshatra about once a day, so the birth time matters.

    Use

    Reading the timetable

    1. Set the date and your city on the page. Times depend on local sunrise and sunset.
    2. Set your bird with the calculator or the pakshi setting, so the Relation column is filled in for you.
    3. Traditionally the best times are slots marked Self or Friend where the activity is Ruling or Eating and the rating is Good or Very Good.
    4. Treat Sleeping and Dying slots, and slots rated Bad or Very Bad, as the weak times.
    5. For times after midnight, check the date suffix. That slot still belongs to the Panchang day that began at sunrise.

    This is a traditional timing system and not a forecast. OnlineJyotish, which publishes these timings, warns against treating them as a guarantee of financial, legal or medical outcomes.
